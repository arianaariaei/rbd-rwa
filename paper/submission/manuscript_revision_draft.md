**Title:** The recording system's noise floor biases automated scoring of REM sleep without atonia across sites

## Statement of Significance

Automated scoring of REM sleep without atonia could find people at risk of Parkinson's disease in routine sleep studies and let research pool patients across centers. Automated implementations of the published background-relative criteria judge chin activity against a multiple of each recording's own noise. As a result, the same rules flagged 9% of patients at one hospital and 29% at another, flagged most healthy volunteers recorded on quiet equipment, and lowered tonic activity in patients with RBD by two-thirds of the difference that predicted Parkinson's disease. Subtracting each recording's noise removed most of this, but makes thresholds depend on amplifier gain. Automated RWA results should be reported together with the recording's noise floor and gain.

## Abstract

**Study Objectives:** To test whether published cut-offs for REM sleep without atonia (RWA), applied by automated scoring, give comparable results on different polysomnography recording systems, and why.

**Methods:** Automated implementations of the Montreal and SINBAR chin criteria and the REM Atonia Index were applied to 1,354 openly available polysomnograms from six recording systems, including 21 patients with RBD. Noise was added to the same nights in steps; a floor-corrected rule subtracted each recording's noise instead of multiplying it; muscle activity of known size was added to nights from every system; three systems were held back for confirmation.

**Results:** The published Montreal criteria flagged 29% of patients at one hospital and 9% at another (difference 20.5 points, 95% CI ⟦⟧). Raising the first hospital's noise floor to the second's lowered positivity from 34% to 10% on the same nights, and RBD sensitivity from 90% to 57%. With known activity, the amplitude a published rule needed to detect rose in proportion to the floor on all six systems (log-log slope ⟦⟧). The floor-corrected rule removed this dependence (slope ⟦⟧) and made the hospitals ⟦equivalent within ±10 points⟧, but its thresholds depend on gain. On held-back systems, published SINBAR flagged 91% of healthy volunteers, floor-corrected 35%. On RBD nights, the noisier floor lowered tonic RWA by 21 points.

**Conclusions:** Automated background-relative RWA scores measure the recording's noise floor as well as the patient. Thresholds should be reported with the floor and gain, and multi-center research should account for the recording system.

**Keywords:** REM Sleep Behavior Disorder; Sleep, REM; Polysomnography; Electromyography; Signal-To-Noise Ratio; Reproducibility of Results; Multicenter Studies as Topic; Parkinson Disease; Synucleinopathies

## Introduction

Isolated REM sleep behavior disorder (RBD) is the strongest known prodromal marker of the alpha-synucleinopathies: in an international multicenter cohort of 1,280 patients, 73.5% developed Parkinson's disease, dementia with Lewy bodies or multiple system atrophy within 12 years \[1\]. People with RBD are the natural population for disease-modifying prevention trials, and there is interest in finding them at scale, for example by screening polysomnograms recorded for other reasons \[2\].

The diagnosis rests on REM sleep without atonia (RWA), documented by video-polysomnography \[3\]. Several scoring methods with diagnostic cut-offs exist: the Montreal method \[4\], the SINBAR method \[5\], the rules of the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) \[6\] and the REM Atonia Index (RAI) \[7,8\]. The visual methods count chin activity that exceeds a multiple of the "background" EMG, judged by eye. Automated implementations make them applicable to thousands of recordings \[9-11,43\], and normative values have been reported from a single center \[12\].

That the EMG noise of a recording affects amplitude measures is not new. Ferri and colleagues built a correction for it into the RAI, subtracting a moving minimum of the rectified amplitude so that the index reflects muscle activity rather than background \[8\], and Levendowski et al. suggested sensor placement as a reason why automated cut-offs differed between laboratory and home recordings \[16\]. What has not been measured is how the background-relative criteria (Montreal, SINBAR and the tools derived from them) behave across recording systems, where the background itself differs. The question is practical. Optimal thresholds in Japanese patients were less than half the Western ones \[14,15\], a difference discussed in terms of ethnicity, referral selection and scoring practice \[39\]; multi-center prodromal cohorts pool RWA measured in different laboratories \[17,18\]; and Parkinson's disease research uses the amount of RWA as a continuous marker \[19-21\], with polysomnographic RWA an indicative biomarker of dementia with Lewy bodies \[22\]. Each use assumes that an RWA percentage means the same on every recording system.

We applied automated implementations of the published criteria, through one open pipeline, to openly available polysomnograms from six recording systems. Three databases (CAP, which holds the only open recordings of patients with RBD; HMC; MGH) were used to find and explain the difference between sites; three further systems were held back to confirm it. We tested the explanation by changing the same recordings rather than by comparing populations: adding noise in steps, adding muscle activity of known size, and subtracting each recording's noise from the criteria.

## Methods

### Study design and data

This retrospective study used openly licensed polysomnography (PSG) databases that need no application (Table 1). All recordings were de-identified by their providers, and the study involved no contact with participants. It was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and is reported following STARD 2015 \[23\] (Supplementary Table S12). \[Institutional statement: e.g. "The \[institution\] ethics committee confirmed that secondary analysis of openly available de-identified data does not require approval (reference ...)".\]

**Discovery cohorts.** CAP (CAP Sleep Database \[24,25\]) supplied the positive class: 22 patients with clinically diagnosed RBD recorded in Parma, Italy, 21 of them on the database's main recording system (chin EMG at 512 Hz in microvolts), together with 51 patients with other sleep disorders on the same system ("same-system patients": nocturnal frontal lobe epilepsy, periodic limb movements, insomnia, narcolepsy). Because a comparison across systems would confound disease with equipment, these 51 are the primary comparison group. CAP's 15 healthy volunteers and ⟦17⟧ further patients were recorded on other systems and are reported only in the Supplement (Table 1, footnote). Two clinical cohorts supplied populations in which RBD is not labeled: HMC (Haaglanden Medisch Centrum sleep staging database, version 1.1; patients referred with a range of sleep complaints, the Netherlands \[26,27\]) and MGH (training set of the PhysioNet/Computing in Cardiology Challenge 2018; Massachusetts General Hospital, USA \[28\]).

**Confirmation cohorts**, processed only after their analysis had been specified: BOAS (100 adults from the general population without psychotropic medication; Micromed SD plus; each participant's first night \[29\]), and from the Dreem Open Datasets \[30\], DOD-O (55 patients with suspected sleep-disordered breathing, Stanford, USA; Somnomedics Somno HD) and DOD-H (25 healthy volunteers, France; Compumedics Siesta).

CAP does not state how RBD was diagnosed. Because a clinical diagnosis requires polysomnographic RWA, sensitivity estimates are subject to incorporation and spectrum bias and are upper bounds for a screening population. We therefore use the RBD recordings to demonstrate a mechanism, not to estimate diagnostic accuracy.

### Signal processing and inclusion

Recordings were streamed from the public repositories, processed in the cloud and discarded; only derived envelopes were kept. The chin EMG was band-pass filtered between 10 and 100 Hz (fourth-order zero-phase Butterworth; upper edge 5 Hz below Nyquist where lower), with notch filters at the mains frequency and its harmonics. Cardiac artifact was removed by subtracting a median heartbeat template (-100 to +150 ms around ECG R-peaks, re-estimated in 5-min blocks; on the chin EMG itself where no ECG was recorded). The signal was reduced to root-mean-square (RMS) and mean-rectified amplitude in 20-ms bins. Sleep stages came from each database's expert scoring. Nights with less than 10 min of REM sleep were excluded (participant flow, Supplementary Figure S1).

### Automated RWA criteria

Four published methods were implemented on the same REM epochs (Table 2): the Montreal criteria (tonic activity in at least 30% of 20-s epochs, or phasic activity in at least 15% of 2-s mini-epochs \[4\]); the SINBAR chin criterion ("any" activity in at least 18.2% of 3-s mini-epochs \[5\]); the AASM rules, for which no cut-off is published and which are therefore reported only as continuous measures (Supplement) \[6\]; and the RAI (below 0.8 \[7,8\]). For the Montreal phasic bursts, sources give a maximum duration of 5 s or 10 s ⟦original: to be confirmed⟧; the primary analysis allowed bursts of up to 15 s, and the results with 5-s and 10-s limits are reported (Supplementary Table ⟦⟧). Absolute criteria (the 10-µV tonic alternative and the RAI) were applied only to recordings in physical units.

The visual methods count activity above a multiple k of the background (k = 2 for tonic and SINBAR activity, k = 4 for Montreal phasic bursts). We operationalized the background as the 5th percentile of 1-s RMS amplitudes in REM sleep, the night's **quiet-REM level (b)**. In a night with intact atonia the quietest seconds contain little muscle activity, so b is set mainly by the noise of electrodes and amplifier; we use "noise floor" only in that sense, and not for patients with RBD, in whom b also contains muscle activity. Alternative definitions of background are reported in the Supplement. These are automated implementations: they reproduce the published rules as written, not a human scorer who judges background by eye and discards artifacts. Against published visual values in RBD, our SINBAR "any" percentage agreed (73% vs 67% \[5\]) but our SINBAR phasic percentage was higher (69.0% vs 40.9%).

### Same-night manipulations

**Noise ladder.** All CAP recordings in physical units, all HMC recordings and the first 300 MGH records were streamed again, and band-limited white noise was added to each night's chin EMG after filtering, at 0.5-3.0 µV RMS and at the level that raised its quiet-REM level to 1.5 µV (HMC's median) or 2.0 µV. The same nights were then raised to 1.5 and 2.0 µV with three other kinds of noise: pink (1/f) noise, white noise whose level drifted slowly through the night, and noise recorded on another system (the quietest REM seconds of 30 HMC nights). One set of MGH nights is used throughout: the ⟦V8: n⟧ with at least 10 min of REM as recorded and in every copy.

**Known muscle activity.** To test the mechanism without relying on the RBD patients, muscle activity of known size was added to the REM sleep of every night without RBD on the six systems (⟦n⟧ nights): tonic episodes of whole 30-s epochs and phasic bursts of 0.25-2 s, made of band-limited Gaussian noise passed through each night's own filter, at 49 amplitudes from 0.25 to 16 µV. For each night and rule we estimated the amplitude at which half of the injected scoring units were detected, corrected for detections in units without added activity (A50). Predictions for white noise came from simulation and were fixed in advance. In a second arm, the REM chin EMG of the 21 RBD patients was added to the REM sleep of these nights (each patient to nights of every system, at full and half amplitude), giving RBD activity on six systems' real backgrounds.

### Floor-corrected criteria

If the background is noise of RMS n, a rule "activity above k times b" demands muscle activity of about √(k² − 1)·b on top of the noise, so the requirement scales with the noise floor. In the floor-corrected (FC) version, activity counts above

```latex
T = \max\left(\sqrt{n^{2} + (k^{2}-1)\,B^{2}},\; g\,b\right), \qquad n = b / 0.91
```

where n is the noise implied by b (for band-limited white noise the 5th percentile of 1-s RMS values is 0.91 times the RMS), B is a reference background (1.0 µV, fixed in advance) and g = 1.5 guards against counting noise. The rule demands of every recording the muscle activity that the published rule demands on a system whose background is B; at b = B it is within 3% of the published rule. Everything else, including the percentage cut-offs, is unchanged. It applies to recordings in physical units at 200 Hz or more with b at most 1.69 µV. Because b contains muscle activity in RBD, the analysis was repeated with the noise estimated from the quiet N2/N3 level, which cannot contain REM muscle activity. Because FC thresholds are partly absolute, they assume a comparable gain; we measured each night's median N2/N3 amplitude with the noise removed in power ("net N2/N3 level") as a check.

RBDtector, an open-source scorer developed for SINBAR scoring in patients with Parkinson's disease and RBD \[10\] and validated at a second center \[11\], was run unchanged through its own controller (commit 7c9c73d), with and without a floor-corrected baseline (Supplementary Section S6). Our runner was verified against the program as released on 12 nights ⟦R5a⟧, and MGH nights were also scored with their annotated arousals.

### Analysis plans

The noise ladder, the floor-corrected criteria, the confirmation cohorts, the Parkinson's analyses, the known-activity experiment and the analyses added in revision were each written down and committed to the project's version-controlled repository before they were run (commits 6d3cc6b, 20b9ec2, 48053f3, 1e7acf0, 09393b4 and 41252ad; Supplementary Sections S3-S8). The discovery analyses that preceded them, and the design of the floor-corrected rule, used the discovery cohorts; only the confirmation cohorts and the known-activity experiment are independent of what was seen. The repository, with its full history, is public with an archived snapshot (Zenodo DOI ⟦⟧), so that the commit order can be checked.

### Statistical analysis

Shares flagged positive are given with 95% Wilson intervals; for CAP RBD they are sensitivities, for groups without RBD false-positive rates (upper bounds in clinical cohorts, where RBD is not labeled). Differences between sites are given with 95% Newcombe hybrid-score intervals; agreement between sites was claimed only where two one-sided tests showed equivalence within ±10 percentage points (90% interval inside the margin). Same-night changes used exact McNemar tests and paired intervals. Slopes of log A50 on log b were estimated by least squares with bootstrap intervals over nights. Rank correlations across groups are Spearman's with exact permutation p. Pre-specified criteria are reported as written, met or not; criteria judged on point estimates are descriptive. Analyses used Python 3 with NumPy, SciPy, pandas and matplotlib; the pipeline runs end to end from one Google Colab notebook.

## Results

### Do published cut-offs give the same answer at different sites?

Table 1 describes the 1,177 discovery and 177 confirmation nights. Of the 21 RBD patients on CAP's main system, the published Montreal and SINBAR cut-offs flagged 19 and 20 (90% \[71-97\]; 95% \[77-99\]) and the RAI 12 (57% \[37-76\]). In patients without RBD, positivity depended more on the hospital than on the method (Figure 1; Table 3): the Montreal criteria flagged 29% \[27-32\] of MGH and 9% \[5-15\] of HMC patients (difference 20.5 points, 95% CI ⟦⟧), SINBAR 76% \[73-79\] and 25% \[19-33\] (51.0 points, ⟦⟧), and the RAI 9% \[7-11\] and 7% \[4-13\] (⟦⟧). CAP's same-system patients were flagged at 33% (Montreal) and 75% (SINBAR). Where positive calls were common the methods disagreed: on 70% of MGH nights against 22% of HMC nights.

### Why not? The quiet-REM level, changed on the same nights

The quiet-REM level differed 2.6-fold between the hospitals: 0.59 µV \[IQR 0.47-0.77\] in MGH and 1.52 µV \[1.23-1.79\] in HMC (Figure 2A). Adding noise to the same MGH nights reproduced the difference. On the ⟦V8: n⟧ MGH nights of the ladder, raising the quiet-REM level to HMC's lowered the share flagged by the Montreal criteria from ⟦34⟧% to ⟦10⟧% (⟦⟧ points \[⟦⟧\]; ⟦65⟧ nights changed, all from positive to negative; McNemar p ⟦5 × 10⁻²⁰⟧) and by SINBAR from ⟦83⟧% to ⟦36⟧%, close to HMC's 9% and 25%; the RAI, which subtracts its own noise estimate, barely moved (⟦⟧). The noisy nights took on HMC's signature (the ratio of the quietest N2/N3 to the quietest REM level fell from 1.20 to 1.08, HMC's value), and HMC's system was noisier on an EEG channel too (40-90 Hz floor 0.46 µV against MGH's 0.23 µV). Along the ladder, positivity fell steadily in every group without RBD, and MGH's curve passed through HMC's point (Montreal 8.9% at HMC's level against HMC's 8.8%; Figure 3). The AASM's continuous measures fell the same way (⟦V9⟧). The dose-response did not depend on the noise being white: at 1.5 µV, pink, drifting and recorded noise flagged ⟦R1a⟧ (Supplementary Table ⟦⟧).

The same mechanism cost sensitivity. CAP's RBD patients were flagged by the Montreal criteria on 90% of nights as recorded (quiet-REM level 0.86 µV), 57% at 1.5 µV and 19% at 3.0 µV; SINBAR held at 95% up to 1.7 µV.

With muscle activity of known size (Figure 4), the amplitude the published rules needed to detect half of the injected units rose in proportion to each night's quiet-REM level on every system: log-log slope ⟦A1: Montreal tonic, phasic, SINBAR any⟧, against 1.00 predicted for white noise. Between the quietest and the noisiest system the required amplitude differed ⟦A3⟧-fold. Real backgrounds behaved ⟦A4: like / unlike⟧ white noise at the same level (⟦⟧% of nights within ±25% of the prediction). Transplanted onto other systems' nights, the real activity of the 21 RBD patients was flagged by the Montreal criteria on ⟦B1: BOAS⟧% of the quietest system's nights and ⟦B1: HMC⟧% of the noisiest's (difference ⟦⟧ \[⟦⟧\]), in line with the ladder (⟦B2⟧).

Other explanations accounted for little (Supplement): storing HMC in MGH's format (1-µV steps, 200 Hz) or scaling it to MGH's amplitude left the relative criteria unchanged; the apnea gradient in MGH disappeared when REM epochs with respiratory events or arousals were excluded; age was not associated with a Montreal or RAI call; and nights with more residual heartbeat were not flagged more often.

### Does subtracting the noise fix it, and does it hold on new systems?

The floor-corrected rules removed most of the dependence on noise. On the same MGH nights, raising the floor to HMC's left Montreal-FC positivity at ⟦V8⟧ (paired difference ⟦⟧) and moved SINBAR-FC from ⟦⟧ to ⟦⟧, whereas the published rules fell by 24 and 47 points. With known activity, the floor-corrected amplitude requirement was nearly independent of the quiet-REM level (slope ⟦A2⟧). As recorded, Montreal-FC flagged 18% \[16-21\] of MGH and 16% \[11-23\] of HMC patients (difference 1.6 points, 95% CI ⟦⟧; ⟦equivalent within ±10 points / equivalence not shown⟧), and SINBAR-FC 45% \[42-49\] and 43% \[35-51\] (2.4 points, ⟦⟧). On the ⟦⟧% of HMC nights within the rule's range, the differences were ⟦R3⟧. With the noise estimated from N2/N3 instead of REM, RBD sensitivity under Montreal-FC was ⟦R2a⟧ (REM estimate: 81%) and the site differences were ⟦⟧.

Floor correction trades dependence on noise for dependence on gain. Scaling HMC by 0.39 left the published rules unchanged but lowered SINBAR-FC positivity from 42.9% to 11.6%. After removing the noise, MGH, HMC and CAP's same-system patients recorded N2/N3 muscle activity at the same scale (net levels 1.60, 1.70 and 1.61 µV), but the healthy cohorts' net level was 0.65-0.66 times MGH's, which may reflect population or gain (electrode spacing and placement); the data cannot tell them apart.

On the three confirmation systems, the published criteria flagged most healthy volunteers on quiet equipment: BOAS (quiet-REM level 0.37 µV) SINBAR 91% \[84-95\] and Montreal 41% \[32-51\], in the 80 BOAS nights without residual heartbeat 92% and 44%; DOD-H (0.56 µV) 84% \[65-94\] and 20% \[9-39\] (Figure 5). Floor-corrected, BOAS fell to 35% \[27-45\] and 15% \[9-24\] and DOD-H to 32% \[17-52\] and 12% \[4-30\]; with 25 DOD-H nights, the difference between the two healthy cohorts (⟦⟧) is too imprecise to show equivalence. DOD-O's patients fell where MGH's ladder predicted for their floor (Montreal 30% against 21%; SINBAR 59% against 62%). Across the six groups without RBD, published positivity fell with the group's quiet-REM level (Spearman ρ ⟦-0.83⟧, exact p ⟦⟧, n = 6) and floor-corrected positivity did not (ρ ⟦⟧, p ⟦⟧); with six groups this is descriptive.

### Consequences for Parkinson's disease research

On the same 21 RBD nights, raising the quiet-REM level from 0.86 to 1.5 µV lowered published Montreal tonic RWA by 21.4 points \[13.2-29.7\] (53.3% to 31.8%; Figure 6A); floor-corrected, it moved by 1.1 points \[-1.7 to -0.5\]. RBDtector, run unchanged, behaved the same: its tonic percentage fell by 12.1 points \[7.3-17.0\], and in people without RBD its verdict followed the recording system (65% \[55-74\] of 100 MGH and 20% \[15-28\] of HMC patients; 89% \[81-94\] of BOAS's healthy volunteers). With its floor-corrected baseline, MGH and HMC were flagged at 55% and 56% and RBD sensitivity stayed at 95% (Figure 6B-C). ⟦R5: arousals, heartbeat⟧. One of ten predictions was not met: floor-corrected RBDtector flagged 49.0% of CAP's same-system patients at 1.5 µV against 37.3% as recorded (Supplementary Section S6). Isolated RWA defined by the RAI (below 0.85, as in the DREAMER cohort \[18\]) applied to 8-17% of nights without RBD in every cohort and hardly moved with added noise, whereas the relative criteria labeled between 9% and 96% of comparable people depending on the system.

## Discussion

Applied through one automated pipeline, the published background-relative RWA criteria flagged a large and strongly site-dependent share of people without RBD, and four lines of evidence trace this to the recording system's noise floor: (1) adding noise to the same nights reproduced the difference between hospitals along a dose-response, with any kind of noise; (2) muscle activity of known size added to nights from six systems was detected only above an amplitude proportional to each night's quiet-REM level; (3) the noisier hospital's floor was noisy on another channel and had the signature of noise; and (4) three systems held back for confirmation behaved as the discovery systems predicted. A correction that subtracts each recording's noise removed most of the dependence on noise, at the price of a dependence on gain. On the same RBD nights, a noisier floor changed tonic RWA by an amount on the scale of the differences Parkinson's disease research relies on.

### What the finding is, and what it is not

The finding concerns automated implementations of background-relative criteria. When "background" is computed as the quietest EMG of the night, a threshold of k times background is, on an atonic night, k times the recording system's noise: a quiet system lets the small twitches of normal REM sleep count, and a noisy one hides part of a patient's activity. The criteria's authors defined background by eye, and our automated scores in people without RBD were three to five times those of visually scored controls \[5\], with a higher phasic percentage in RBD than published. A visual scorer reads chin EMG at a chosen display gain at which twitches of a microvolt or two are barely visible, which may protect visual scoring from the mechanism. We have specified a blinded test (the same nights scored visually as recorded and with added noise; protocol registered at ⟦OSF⟧) and do not claim that visual scoring shares the effect.

Floor correction is a correction for noise, not a complete remedy. Its thresholds are partly absolute and therefore move with amplifier gain and electrode placement: scaling one hospital's recordings by 0.39 changed floor-corrected but not published positivity. Its noise estimate, taken from the quietest REM seconds, also contains muscle activity in patients with RBD (quiet-REM level 0.86 against 0.48 µV in same-system patients), so it subtracts too much in the patients with most tone, which may explain the lower Montreal-FC sensitivity; an estimate from N2/N3 ⟦R2⟧. It should not be read below 200 Hz or above a quiet-REM level of 1.69 µV, which excluded ⟦⟧% of HMC nights, and noise that drifts through the night ⟦R1b drift⟧. The RAI, which subtracts a moving-minimum noise estimate \[8\], was stable across systems and nearly immune to added noise but, like any absolute measure, depends on gain. Two numbers computable from any recording would make automated RWA results comparable: the quiet-REM level (noise) and the net N2/N3 level (gain). We provide a tool that reports both.

### Relation to previous work

Because the thresholds follow the floor, a single published cut-off has a different operating point on each recording system. Equipment is therefore one candidate explanation, untested, for differences between published thresholds. In Japanese patients with isolated RBD, the optimal threshold for the combined SINBAR measure (chin "any" with flexor digitorum superficialis phasic activity, scored visually) was 11.3% rather than 27.2% \[14,15\]; the authors and an editorial discussed referral selection, younger controls, ethnicity and scoring practice \[14,39\], but the noise floor of the recording system was not reported, so its contribution cannot be judged. Levendowski et al.'s laboratory and home cut-offs, the latter derived in their study and the former taken from an earlier cohort \[16\], and their suggestion that sensor placement contributes, are consistent with an equipment effect. Single-system comparisons of computerized methods \[9\] and single-center normative values \[12\] could not show the effect, because within one system the floor varies little.

### What this means for Parkinson's disease research

Parkinson's disease research uses the amount of RWA: to estimate who will convert, to follow progression, to define isolated RWA as a possible earlier stage and to pool patients across centers \[17-21\]. The visually scored benchmarks are of the same order as the system effect we measured: baseline tonic chin RWA was 72.9% in RBD patients who later developed Parkinson's disease against 41.0% in those who remained disease-free \[19\]; in 55 patients with isolated RBD, SINBAR cut-offs of 16.4% (tonic) and 36.8% ("any") best predicted early conversion \[40\]; patients above 46.4% "any" RWA converted faster \[41\]; and tonic RWA rose from 30% to 54% over about five years in 11 patients \[20\]. These comparisons concern scale only: the benchmarks were scored visually on other systems and are not called into question. Two further yardsticks: the night-to-night variability of the RAI in RBD is about 20% \[42\], and the system effect on the same nights (21 points of tonic RWA for a 0.6-µV rise in the floor) is larger than that. In multi-center cohorts, part of the variation between patients may therefore reflect where they were recorded; adjusting for center removes this only if each center's floor is stable. In longitudinal studies, a change of amplifier or electrodes during follow-up could, as a scenario, appear as progression or regression; we know of no data on how much equipment changes move the floor in practice. RBDtector inherits the dependence because its baseline is, in effect, the noise floor; its verdicts in established RBD were robust, but its continuous percentages were not. For isolated RWA, DREAMER's RAI-based definition was the most stable we tested. Screening yields are illustrative only (Supplementary Table S11).

### Strengths and limitations

Only openly licensed data and public code were used; the explanation was tested by changing recordings rather than assumed; analyses were written down before they were run and reported whatever the outcome; and the known-activity experiment gives ground truth that does not depend on any patient group.

The limitations are substantial. The only open RBD recordings are CAP's: 21 patients with established RBD from one center and one system. We therefore present them as a demonstration of mechanism; sensitivity estimates are imprecise and optimistic for prodromal cases, and positive predictive values are upper bounds. The known-activity experiment shows that the mechanism does not depend on these patients, but synthetic activity is not RBD, and transplanted activity carries CAP's own noise. No recordings of people with Parkinson's disease were available; nothing in the mechanism is specific to the patients studied, but this was not tested in Parkinson's disease, and we have invited centers holding such recordings to run the open pipeline locally. Visual scoring was not tested (see above). RBD status is unknown in the clinical cohorts, where most but not all positive calls are false; the same-night analyses do not depend on this. Medication is not documented in any cohort; serotonergic antidepressants increase REM chin tone \[31,32\], and if, for example, one patient in five took them and all were flagged, medication could contribute up to 20 points to a false-positive rate, but it cannot explain changes on the same nights. CAP's comparison group was mostly younger than the RBD patients and includes five patients with narcolepsy, a condition with RWA \[33\]; discrimination held against patients aged 50 or over (AUC 0.81-0.88) and without narcolepsy (⟦V7⟧). Only chin EMG was analyzed. Automated scoring did not discard movement, snoring or electrode artifacts. The analysis plans were committed to the authors' own repository; their order can be checked in its public history and archived snapshot, but they were not registered externally, and the floor-corrected rule was designed on the discovery cohorts.

## Conclusions

Automated implementations of the published background-relative RWA criteria do not give comparable results on different recording systems: the same rules flagged 9% of patients at one hospital and 29% at another and most healthy volunteers on quiet equipment. The difference follows the recording's noise floor, against which these criteria, implemented automatically, are in effect measured; the same mechanism makes noisier systems miss RBD activity and moves continuous RWA by amounts on the scale of the effects studied in Parkinson's disease research. Subtracting each recording's noise removes most of this but makes thresholds depend on gain. Automated RWA results should be reported with the recording's noise floor and gain, and studies that pool RWA across centers or years should account for the recording system.

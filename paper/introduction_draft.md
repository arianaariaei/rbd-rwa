# Introduction (draft)

Isolated REM sleep behaviour disorder (RBD) is the strongest known prodromal marker of
the alpha-synucleinopathies: in a multicentre cohort, about three quarters of patients
developed Parkinson's disease, dementia with Lewy bodies or multiple system atrophy
within 12 years [Postuma 2019]. People with RBD are therefore the natural population
for disease-modifying prevention trials, and there is growing interest in finding them
at scale - for example by screening polysomnograms (PSG) recorded for other reasons.

The diagnosis rests on a polysomnographic finding: REM sleep without atonia (RWA), the
loss of the normal muscle paralysis of REM sleep. RWA is quantified from chin (and
sometimes limb) EMG, and several scoring schemes with diagnostic cut-offs exist: the
Montreal method [Montplaisir 2010], the SINBAR method [Frauscher 2012], the AASM
scoring rules, and the REM Atonia Index (RAI) [Ferri 2008, 2010]. Each was developed
and validated in one or a few centres, largely with visual scoring, and each defines
muscle activity relative to a "background" level that a human scorer judges by eye.
Automated implementations [Cesari 2018; Rothenbacher 2022] make these methods
applicable to thousands of recordings, and normative values in adults without RBD have
been reported from a single centre [Feemster 2019].

Three questions matter before RWA can be used to screen large populations, and none
has been answered on shared data:

1. **Do the published cut-offs transfer to automated scoring?** Cut-offs were set on
   visual scores; an automated reading of the same criteria may place the threshold
   differently, above all through its operational definition of background.
2. **Do they transfer across recording sites?** EMG amplitude depends on amplifier
   bandwidth, resolution and electrode montage, which differ between laboratories.
3. **How many people without RBD would they flag?** In a screening setting the
   false-positive rate - driven by age, sleep apnoea, arousals and cardiac
   artefact - determines the positive predictive value far more than sensitivity does.

Here we apply four published RWA methods, through one open and fully automated
pipeline, to 1,250 polysomnograms from three openly available databases on two
continents: the CAP Sleep Database (including 22 patients with RBD), a Dutch clinical
cohort (HMC) and a US clinical cohort (MGH, PhysioNet Challenge 2018). We report how
often each method's cut-off labels patients with and without RBD, how often the
methods disagree on the same night, what drives false positives, and what this
implies for screening. Because the cohorts differ in recording equipment, we then
re-create recordings in software - changing their storage format, amplitude and noise
floor one at a time - to find out which property of a recording moves the verdict, and test,
with criteria fixed in advance, whether subtracting that property makes the published
criteria portable.
Because every dataset and line of code is public, all results can be regenerated by
anyone.
